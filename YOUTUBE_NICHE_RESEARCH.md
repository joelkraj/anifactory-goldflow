# YouTube Niche Research

Last updated: August 1, 2026

## Purpose

This document is the opportunity backlog for new Goldflow YouTube channels and production profiles. It records possible niches, why demand may exist, where competition may be weak, how well each format fits voiceover plus generated visuals, and what evidence is required before production.

The canonical implementation and restart checkpoint for the current top factual lane is [ASSET_AFTERLIFE_CHANNEL_PLAYBOOK.md](ASSET_AFTERLIFE_CHANNEL_PLAYBOOK.md). The separate second-person identity-simulation lane is preserved in [IMMERSIVE_POV_CHANNEL_PLAYBOOK.md](IMMERSIVE_POV_CHANNEL_PLAYBOOK.md).

An entry in this document is not automatically a validated niche. Demand and competition change quickly. Every candidate must pass the live YouTube validation gate below before receiving a channel, production profile, or three-video test.

## Current Market Signals

The broad opportunity is educational entertainment with a strong visual promise, not generic faceless automation.

- YouTube reported more than 5.5 billion United States views on learning and how-to content during June 2025. This supports continued demand for understandable explainers, but does not prove demand for any individual niche.
- Google and Kantar reported that 90 percent of surveyed United States YouTube viewers use the platform to go deeper on topics they care about, 88 percent explore passions and interests, and 87 percent use it to learn and grow.
- Google and Kantar also reported that 69 percent of surveyed United States viewers would rather watch video than read or listen when learning a new skill.
- YouTube reported that 63 percent of surveyed animation fans aged fourteen to twenty-four watch independent animated series made for YouTube at least weekly. This supports selective Imagen and LTX storytelling when the visual identity feels authored.
- YouTube explicitly excludes generic, repetitive, mass-produced content from monetization. A replace-the-noun template is not a defensible channel strategy.

Sources are listed at the end of this document.

## Goldflow Advantage

Goldflow is best suited to niches with all of the following characteristics.

- A voiceover can carry the episode without a host on camera.
- The title can promise a complete story, answer, transformation, or consequence.
- Generated imagery can depict scenes that stock footage cannot show cheaply.
- Each episode contains changing stakes rather than a list of facts.
- Research, continuity, narration, visuals, and packaging can be validated as separate artifacts.
- The subject supports at least thirty meaningfully different episodes without repeating a template.

Goldflow should not compete on upload volume. It should compete on premise selection, narrative construction, visual specificity, and packaging.

## Candidate Scorecard

Score every candidate from one to five in each category. Do not use a total score to hide a fatal weakness.

| Category | One | Five |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Proven demand | Little recent evidence | Multiple recent small-channel outliers |
| Competition gap | Dominated by mature specialists | Weak or inconsistent existing supply |
| Click clarity | Requires explanation | Understandable in one title and image |
| Retention engine | Mostly facts or atmosphere | Built-in objective, stakes, reversals, and answer |
| Episode depth | Fewer than ten credible topics | More than fifty differentiated topics |
| Visual advantage | Generic stock is sufficient | Generated scenes materially improve the promise |
| Production reliability | Heavy rights, identity, or factual risk | Repeatable with source and QA controls |
| Monetization safety | Sensitive, repetitive, or advertiser-hostile | Original, useful, and advertiser-compatible |
| Revenue adjacency | Ads only | Sponsors, affiliates, products, or lead generation fit |

Automatic rejection conditions:

- The channel depends on copied clips, unlicensed media, or another creator's script structure.
- The episode can be produced by changing only a noun, date, place, or celebrity name.
- The premise makes factual claims that cannot be supported by accessible sources.
- The visual plan requires realistic deception involving real people or events.
- The niche needs daily trend chasing to remain relevant.
- The economics require high upload volume before quality signals can appear.

## Established Formats Worth Testing

### The Secret Afterlife Of Expensive Things

Internally, call this format **Asset Afterlife**. Each episode follows a valuable object after its final public moment and answers where it goes, who owns it, what continuing liabilities it creates, what can still be sold, and whether it is repaired, repurposed, dismantled, or destroyed.

The viewer promise is mystery plus money plus hidden logistics plus visible transformation. The emotional treatment should feel like an investigation, funeral, and possible resurrection rather than an encyclopedia entry. The object is the episode's protagonist.

Every approved premise must contain all six elements:

1. A high-value or high-scale subject.
2. A terminal event such as retirement, abandonment, seizure, loss, shutdown, repossession, wreck, or failure to sell.
3. A hidden chain of custody showing ownership, liability, storage, and control.
4. A consequential decision tree such as repair, auction, export, part-out, salvage, recycling, or destruction.
5. A visible physical transformation.
6. A final verdict explaining what survived, what was lost, who paid, who profited, and where the asset ultimately went.

This is not a generic recycling, factory-process, abandoned-places, graveyard, or noun-swapped what-happens-to channel. Reject premises that lack a meaningful ownership mystery, economic decision, physical journey, or final answer.

Approved controlled three-video test:

1. **Where Lost Luggage Goes When Nobody Claims It** is the lowest-risk first script and tests personal value, tracing, compensation, custody, sorting, resale, donation, and destruction.
2. **What Happens to a Passenger Jet After Its Final Flight?** tests the strongest demonstrated asset class through storage, ownership, engines, certified parts, dismantling, recycling, and possible return to service.
3. **The Five-Year Afterlife of the Costa Concordia** tests a named, unusually well-documented salvage and dismantling case. Frame it around engineering and disposition rather than human tragedy.

Hold **What Happens to a Subway Car After Its Last Ride?** until a current agency disposition contract identifies the buyer, hazardous-material procedure, transport method, preservation split, and final destination. Do not infer current practice from older artificial-reef programs.

Initial episodes should target ten to fourteen minutes:

- Open with the asset's final flight, voyage, route, sale attempt, or operating shift.
- Establish why it cannot simply disappear: owner, lender, insurer, regulator, hazards, storage, and immediate cost.
- Follow the hidden physical and legal journey into storage, inspection, auction, reclamation, or dismantling.
- Reveal the component, material, land right, or inventory with unexpected remaining value.
- Force a documented repair-versus-disposal decision or comparable obstacle.
- Show the transformation and end with the final destination, recovery, loss, and beneficiary.

Packaging should use one hero asset, one obvious before-and-after transition, one arrow or dividing line, one visibly missing or transformed component, and no more than two or three thumbnail words. Useful labels include `FINAL FLIGHT`, `NO OWNER`, `LAST STOP`, `CUT APART`, `NEVER SOLD`, `LEFT BEHIND`, and `WORTH MORE DEAD`.

The fatal risk is confidently fabricating a process. Every episode must identify its jurisdiction and time period, distinguish industry-standard practice from one documented case, source ownership and economic claims, label estimates, and prevent synthetic visuals from presenting impossible machinery or invented workflows as fact.

### Premium Anime What If Timelines

Ten-to-thirty-minute counterfactual stories built from one explicit canon divergence. The episode must show the immediate consequence, second-order effects, changed relationships, altered fights, new costs, and a concrete final timeline rather than merely describing who becomes stronger.

The production moat is not access to character names. It is canon literacy, causal reasoning, longform story construction, character consistency, and premium original visual direction. Joel's prior experience in this lane is a material operating advantage and raises this format above otherwise less crowded but unfamiliar niches.

Promising premise families:

- A different protagonist or vessel.
- A mentor survives, arrives earlier, or fails at a decisive moment.
- A rival wins the canonical turning-point battle.
- A character accepts the leadership role they originally rejected.
- A sealed, exiled, or dead character remains active through later arcs.
- Two existing abilities, alliances, or responsibilities change owners.
- One canonical promise is kept instead of broken.

Premise quality is franchise-specific. A famous character is not enough. Select branch points where viewers already debate the consequence and where the changed choice creates at least three downstream reversals.

Do not use anime episodes, manga panels, soundtrack recordings, official voice performances, or cloned actor voices as the production substrate. Original narration and generated visuals reduce direct asset reuse, but they do not eliminate the underlying derivative-work copyright risk.

### Betrayal And Revenge Manhwa

Current Goldflow lane. Longform fictional narration with a concrete betrayal, earned reversal, betrayer loss, and final boundary. It has proven audience demand but high script-length and continuity costs.

### POV, Your Life As

Six-to-twelve-minute second-person experiential stories. The viewer receives an identity, objective, constraints, escalating choices, and a complete life or shift.

Promising sub-niches:

- Your life as a medieval castle guard.
- Your life as a plague-era gravedigger.
- Your life as a Roman bath attendant.
- Your life as a lighthouse keeper during a winter storm.
- Your life as a telegraph operator during a national emergency.
- Your life as a railway fireman before diesel engines.
- Your life as a palace food taster.
- Your life as a deep-sea cable repair worker.

Avoid beginning with kings, gladiators, Vikings, samurai, or generic peasants unless live research reveals a new packaging angle. Those subjects already attract abundant supply.

### The Economics Of Owning X

Eight-to-fifteen-minute operator-economics stories. Each episode follows startup cost, fixed costs, variable costs, customer behavior, operational surprises, break-even, failure conditions, and a final verdict.

Competition warning: this format is receiving substantial attention from YouTube automation operators. Do not launch a generic economics-of channel. Reconsider it only when live research finds a narrow subject cluster with proven demand, weak current supply, and information or access competitors cannot cheaply reproduce.

Promising subjects:

- A twenty-four-hour gym.
- A self-service car wash.
- A laundromat.
- A climbing gym.
- A storage facility.
- A funeral home.
- A bowling alley.
- An indoor playground.
- A campground.
- A food truck commissary.
- A wedding venue.
- A pet boarding facility.
- A small marina.
- A skating rink.
- A vending-machine route.

Do not present a hypothetical model as a universal profit claim. State the market, year, assumptions, and sensitivity range.

### Rise And Fall

Ten-to-twenty-minute causal histories of a business, product category, venue, technology, or public project. Prefer overlooked subjects with a clear reason for growth and a different reason for collapse.

### Why X Is So Expensive

Eight-to-fourteen-minute cost-chain explainers. The strongest episodes follow an object or service through labor, materials, regulation, logistics, spoilage, risk, margin, and consumer price.

### Hidden Process And Supply Chain

Eight-to-fifteen-minute journeys through systems viewers use but rarely see. The object or service must physically travel and encounter bottlenecks so the visual story changes throughout the episode.

### How The World Worked Before

Ten-to-eighteen-minute operational history. Each episode begins with a familiar modern need, removes the technology viewers take for granted, and reconstructs the people, machines, schedules, risks, and dependencies that solved the problem.

Promising subjects:

- How a city got ice before refrigerators.
- How hotels woke hundreds of guests before alarm clocks.
- How milk reached cities before refrigerated trucks.
- How elevators worked before electric motors.
- How cities fought fires before modern hydrants.
- How news crossed an ocean before radio.
- How office buildings moved messages before telephones.
- How skyscrapers were cleaned before powered platforms.

The barrier is the product. A credible episode requires period manuals, patents, maps, photographs, occupational records, and a consistent visual reconstruction. Generic stock footage cannot deliver the same experience.

### Cascading Infrastructure Failure

Twelve-to-eighteen-minute researched simulations showing what fails first, what survives, which safeguards activate, and how recovery changes the outcome. Every episode needs a defined place or system, starting condition, timeline, dependency map, and evidence-backed uncertainty range.

Promising subjects:

- What happens when a city loses water for seventy-two hours.
- How a hospital survives a twenty-four-hour blackout.
- The first forty-eight hours after a major port shuts down.
- What happens when a city's sewage pumps stop.
- Which systems fail first if satellite navigation disappears.
- How one transformer failure can cascade across a city.
- What protects a subway when floodwater reaches the entrances.

Avoid apocalypse promises. The editorial payoff is understanding real safeguards and failure chains, not frightening viewers with unsupported collapse claims.

## New Lower-Competition Angles

The following are narrower intersections designed to reduce direct competition while preserving broad curiosity.

### Business Economics Plus A Crisis

Instead of merely asking whether a business makes money, test it against a specific high-pressure event.

- Can a gym survive losing January memberships?
- What happens when a laundromat loses water for one day?
- Can a wedding venue survive a rain season?
- How does a marina prepare for a hurricane evacuation?
- Why one broken freezer can destroy a convenience store's month.

### Infrastructure Plus A Human Objective

Avoid detached system tours. Give the episode a deadline and a person-dependent outcome.

- How a city keeps drinking water running during a blackout.
- How one airport clears a runway before the next landing.
- Where hospital waste goes before sunrise.
- How a stadium empties seventy thousand people without gridlock.
- What protects a subway when the river rises.

### History Plus A Shift Or Career

Cover one working day, promotion path, disaster, or final shift rather than an entire civilization.

- The final shift inside a coal-fired ocean liner.
- The night a lighthouse keeper lost the lamp.
- How a medieval town survived when its only mill stopped.
- The last telegraph operator in a city that had moved to telephones.
- What happened when an ice-delivery route met the refrigerator.

### Design Plus A Failure Case

Explain a familiar design choice by first showing the consequence of getting it wrong.

- Why hospital doors open the way they do.
- Why parking garages spiral instead of stacking straight ramps.
- Why airport baggage systems avoid perfect symmetry.
- Why grocery stores place refrigeration around the edges.
- Why fire stairs feel uncomfortable on purpose.

### Price Breakdown Plus A Verdict

Give the viewer a defensible conclusion rather than a list of costs.

- What a concert ticket actually pays for.
- Why bowling costs more with fewer bowlers.
- Why a storage unit can cost more than the objects inside it.
- Why airport food is expensive, and which costs are real.
- Why cheap gyms need members who rarely visit.

## Live Validation Gate

Every proposed niche must receive a dated research packet before a test is approved. Use public YouTube results, current channel analytics where relevant, Google Trends where useful, and direct inspection of channels and videos. Search-engine article lists are idea sources, not proof.

### Research Sample

Collect at least the following:

- Ten distinct search phrases representing the niche and its adjacent viewer intent.
- The first twenty relevant videos for each phrase, with duplicates removed.
- At least thirty relevant videos published within the previous twelve months.
- At least ten relevant videos published within the previous ninety days when available.
- Channel subscribers, video views, publish date, duration, title, thumbnail pattern, and channel upload frequency.
- At least five comments from each of five strong videos, classified by what viewers wanted next.

### Core Measurements

Calculate these values rather than relying on intuition.

- Views per day since publication.
- Views divided by channel subscribers.
- Median views for the channel's previous ten comparable uploads.
- Video views divided by that channel median, used as the breakout multiple.
- Number of active channels publishing the exact format at least twice per month.
- Recent supply, measured as relevant uploads during the last thirty and ninety days.
- Demand concentration, measured as the share of relevant views owned by the top three channels.
- Packaging convergence, measured by repeated title structures and thumbnail compositions.
- Comment demand, measured by repeated requests for adjacent episodes.

### Initial Pass Conditions

A candidate may advance to a three-video test when all of the following are true.

- At least three relevant videos from the last twelve months achieved a breakout multiple of three or greater.
- At least one breakout came from a channel with fewer than two hundred fifty thousand subscribers.
- At least two relevant videos from the last ninety days show meaningful current demand, unless the niche is strongly evergreen and search-led.
- No more than five mature channels completely dominate the exact proposed format.
- The research identifies at least thirty differentiated episode premises.
- Three launch premises each have a distinct title promise and thumbnail composition.
- We can add original analysis, narrative structure, or visual access that current supply lacks.

These thresholds are working heuristics. Record exceptions rather than silently changing them.

## Three-Video Test Design

Do not judge a niche from one upload. Test three different demand surfaces on one channel.

1. Broad familiar subject with immediate click clarity.
2. Narrower low-competition subject with a stronger novelty gap.
3. Hybrid subject that combines the best-performing promise with a new setting.

Keep the format, narrator identity, visual language, duration band, and publishing cadence stable enough that the premise and packaging remain the primary variables.

Record for every upload:

- Impressions and click-through rate after one day, three days, seven days, and twenty-eight days.
- First thirty-second retention.
- Average percentage viewed and average view duration.
- Retention changes at the first reveal, midpoint reversal, answer, and ending.
- Browse, suggested, search, and external traffic shares.
- New versus returning viewers.
- Subscriber conversion per one thousand views.
- Revenue per mille when monetized.
- Thumbnail and title changes with exact timestamps.
- Production cost, active operator time, generation cost, and elapsed turnaround.

Continue only when the evidence identifies a repeatable viewer promise, not merely one lucky topic.

## Channel And Production Rules

- Use a separate channel when the viewer promise, expected audience, or emotional reason to click changes materially.
- Do not mix manhwa betrayal, business economics, historical POV, and infrastructure explainers on one new channel.
- A channel may combine adjacent formats only when the same viewer would plausibly watch all of them. For example, business economics, price breakdowns, and venue crisis economics can share a channel.
- Create a Goldflow production profile only after a niche passes research and its first script demonstrates repeated structural needs.
- Smaller formats may use one manual ChatGPT authoring bundle for premise, outline, script, visual direction, and packaging candidates. Goldflow must import, validate, hash, and approve the resulting artifacts.
- Use LTX selectively. Motion should clarify a process, physical action, transformation, or crisis. Do not animate every still merely because generation is available.
- Generated realistic depictions of actual people, places, or events must receive the appropriate YouTube altered or synthetic content disclosure.
- Every factual format needs a claim ledger with source, observation date, uncertainty, and whether a number is measured, reported, estimated, or hypothetical.
- Every Asset Afterlife episode needs an asset, terminal event, chain of custody, consequential decision tree, visible transformation, and final verdict. Missing one is a premise-level blocker.
- Asset Afterlife claim ledgers must record jurisdiction and year, distinguish standard practice from a documented case, and source ownership, prices, quantities, environmental claims, and final disposition.
- Synthetic Asset Afterlife visuals may illustrate inaccessible steps, but they must not depict impossible machinery, invented economics, or a hypothetical process as documented footage. Label material hypothetical illustrations where needed.
- Keep Asset Afterlife on a dedicated factual-explainer channel. Do not mix it with anime What If or manhwa betrayal storytelling.
- Every anime What If episode needs a canon ledger separating canonical facts, explicit divergence, necessary inference, and original invention.
- Keep anime What If on a dedicated channel. Do not mix it with manhwa recap merely because both use illustrated fantasy visuals.
- Treat monetization eligibility and copyright exposure as separate reviews. A video may be sufficiently original for YouTube's reused-content policy and still receive a copyright claim or legal challenge.
- Frame the episode as original counterfactual analysis and storytelling. Avoid presenting fan invention as leaked, official, canonical, or endorsed material.

## Recommended Attack Order

Use separate channels and treat these as portfolio lanes with different risk profiles.

1. Run the controlled three-video Asset Afterlife test.
2. Run one separate JJK What If pilot while franchise attention remains current.
3. Build the historical-operations and lost-workplace research moat.
4. Finish the cascading-infrastructure validation packet.
5. Hold manhwa and light-novel What If until independent longform demand appears.
6. Reject broad non-franchise What If as an umbrella channel; route strong systems premises into sourced simulations instead.

The lanes approved here are approved for controlled tests, not for a portfolio rollout. Asset Afterlife has not yet proven that one audience follows the promise across unrelated objects, and anime What If remains a mature, high-copyright-risk format.

The current leading controlled-test hypothesis is Asset Afterlife.
