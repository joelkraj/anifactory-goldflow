REALITY AND CLARITY CONTRACT

When something impossible happens, characters must not accept it instantly unless established knowledge explains why.

Include at least one grounded human reaction, failed assumption, physical test, or moment of disbelief. Joey may admit that he does not know the cause while still recognizing the immediate danger.

The narrator must quickly make the practical reality understandable:

What changed.

Who or what is affected.

What can now hurt, kill, reward, or constrain people.

What Joey knows from prior experience.

What Joey still does not know.

Do not bury this orientation in lore or raw system text. State the minimum rule in plain narration, prove it physically, and continue.

Explanation must arrive when the viewer needs it. Introduce one important rule at a time, then show its consequence. Do not assume the viewer understands game terminology merely because Joey does.

When multiple people receive powers, bodies, ranks, memories, or ownership states, explain the governing distinction once in plain language and prove it through visible results.

Do not let the narrator leak future information merely to sound knowing. Avoid lines such as this will matter later, someone not yet introduced will solve it, or the enemy will eventually discover this weakness.

JOEY MANHWA

The protagonist is always named Joey Manhwa.

Joey is the fantasy, but he remains recognizably human. He becomes competent, powerful, respected, desirable, dangerous, or otherwise elevated in ways that fit this premise.

Joey is controlled, decisive, boundary-driven, strategically aggressive, observant, and capable of overwhelming force. His strength comes from what he notices, chooses, prepares, risks, and executes.

He may feel shock, fear, grief, anger, uncertainty, attraction, or relief. Human reactions do not make him weak. He can ask questions, test assumptions, make a limited mistake, and update his plan.

He does not remain passive after receiving clear evidence. He does not repeat an old mistake without immediate cost and permanent learning. He does not accept unrelated contests because someone insults him. He does not hide decisive information from allies without a strategic reason.

The system may expose, measure, teach, constrain, or reward. It must not make Joey's moral or strategic decisions for him.

DIALOGUE CONTRACT

Joey must sound like a real competent adult, not a machine generating comeback quotes.

Use indirect dialogue for routine questions, explanations, planning, and negotiation. Use direct dialogue when the exact words change a relationship, trigger a decision, reveal cruelty, enforce a boundary, or complete a payoff.

Do not give Joey a polished comeback in every confrontation. Do not stack slogans, threats, aphorisms, antithesis, or mic-drop lines. Reserve truly memorable dialogue for a few high-value moments, such as the opening reversal, the central relationship boundary, and the climax.

For a betrayal or revenge story near twenty thousand words, use no more than three deliberately iconic Joey lines. This is not a ban on natural direct dialogue. It is a ban on writing every reply like a quote card.

Joey may say that he does not know. He may ask what happened. He may give short practical instructions. He may answer imperfectly when emotional.

Make Joey cool through decisions and consequences. Do not make him announce how cool he is.

Give other characters distinct motives and natural speech. They should not exist only to feed Joey setup lines.

THE CORE ADVANTAGE

Prefer one simple, unfair core advantage with:

A clear rule.

A visible trigger.

A useful effect.

A real limit, cost, condition, cooldown, scarcity, or strategic constraint.

Multiple applications native to the premise.

Teach the advantage through use. Establish it before it solves a major problem.

For each important mechanic, make four things easy to understand:

The rule.

The immediate danger or opportunity.

The limit or cost.

The choice Joey makes because of it.

The limit must remain binding. Do not create a restriction and then bypass it through a technicality, a different item category, an instant secondary menu, or an unexplained new function.

If Joey owns many powers, items, copied talents, summons, or skills, define what he can actively access and what changing access costs. An enormous collection is not permission to produce the perfect answer without setup.

Track the exact active access state silently through every encounter. If an item, skill, summon, or resource becomes available, unavailable, consumed, damaged, staged, transferred, or placed on cooldown, keep that state continuous until the story explicitly changes it.

Do not use the same solution loop in every encounter. Avoid repeatedly identifying a mechanic, retrieving a newly named perfect counter, explaining its original purpose, and winning.

In a major encounter, introduce no more than one decisive solution that was not previously established. Later reversals should usually come from preparation, an ally's useful skill, the environment, a prior sacrifice, a known item used differently, or Joey accepting a real cost.

Name a special item, skill, or system function only when the name helps the viewer track something that will be reused, sacrificed, countered, or paid off later. Describe disposable one-use tools generically when another proper name would add cognitive load without story value.

PREMISE-NATIVE PROGRESSION

Do not force every story into wealth, empire building, politics, city management, or apocalypse civilization management.

A combat premise should progress through technique, physical ability, opponents, reputation, and combat applications.

A dungeon premise should progress through survival, exploration, combat, loot, allies, and dungeon mastery.

A learning premise should identify the exact knowledge, motor skill, strategy, or technical principle Joey acquires.

A romance premise should progress through boundaries, loyalty, desirability, trust, choices, and status reversal.

A business premise may progress through operations, money, contracts, ownership, leverage, and influence.

Use only the progression this title and premise promise.

Do not default every long story to a doctor, engineer, fortress, water system, city council, public trial, tournament, inventory-suppression field, or regional apocalypse. Use those elements only when the specific premise naturally earns them.

If the premise or operator provides possible set pieces, treat them as options rather than a mandatory chapter list unless they are explicitly marked required. Select only the strongest functions and invent a fresh causal route between them.

Across a long script, change the objective, pressure type, relationship, available resource, or status often enough that each five-to-ten-minute band feels like progression rather than another version of the previous scene.

WRITE MECHANISMS, NOT OUTCOMES

Never skip the interesting part by saying Joey somehow survives, quickly masters a skill, outsmarts everyone, builds something successful, or easily wins.

Show the decisive physical method, learned principle, preparation, trap, resource interaction, sacrifice, or adaptation.

Every major scene follows:

Goal, obstacle, Joey's decision, opposing response, execution or adaptation, consequence.

Each consequence creates the next decision, threat, cost, opportunity, or relationship change.

After a complicated mechanism, state the result and its consequence once. Do not replay the procedure, summarize it twice, or add a paragraph explaining what the scene symbolized.

OPPOSITION, VARIETY, AND SUPPORTING CHARACTERS

Antagonists remain competent. They gather information, adapt after failure, exploit known limits, protect their interests, and retreat when appropriate.

Do not repeat the same conflict with more enemies, louder insults, or another public disbelief reaction. Change the pressure between physical, technical, social, emotional, reputational, strategic, relational, and institutional problems.

After the opening wound, move into the promised adventure. Keep the betrayer's consequences alive without replaying humiliation for the entire runtime.

Supporting characters need practical goals, useful abilities, loyalties, disagreements, and decisions. They may solve parts of problems that fit their expertise. They do not exist only to praise Joey, react in shock, explain the system, or become jealous.

Add romance only when it improves this story. Attraction develops through choices, trust, competence, vulnerability, and shared action rather than instant worship.

REVENGE, CLIMAX, AND ENDING

Revenge must alter something real, such as access, rank, reputation, credibility, authority, leverage, safety, relationships, possessions, opportunities, or the betrayer's future. A speech alone is not revenge.

Give the title promise:

An early physical proof.

A meaningful mid-story expansion.

A final visible payoff.

The climax must resolve the original package using rules, skills, relationships, evidence, costs, and setups established earlier.

Do not introduce an unrelated god, hidden mastermind, former system owner, random final villain, or sequel mystery to manufacture scale.

Make the ending emotionally symmetric. The person who misjudged or exploited Joey faces a consequence connected to that choice, while Joey claims the better life, freedom, relationship, role, or status created by his decisions.

Stay long enough for the payoff to register, then end with a complete local resolution. Do not add generic sequel bait.

By default, keep the primary threat and human-antagonist payoffs active through roughly the final fifth of the story. Aim to resolve the main climax around eighty-four to ninety-two percent of the target words, then use the remaining eight to sixteen percent for direct consequences, the relationship boundary, changed status, and one concise new equilibrium.

Do not spend more than roughly the final fifteen percent on rebuilding, governance, ceremonies, institutional detail, future-history montage, or repeated thematic reflection unless that material is the title's promised payoff.

RECAP CADENCE

Default to connected third-person present-tense recap narration. Use another POV or tense only when it clearly improves this premise, and keep transitions deliberate and stable.

Write for smooth delivery at two hundred ten to two hundred twenty spoken words per minute.

The narrator sounds like an informed person efficiently explaining compelling panels. The voice is urgent, concrete, clear, and emotionally aware without becoming loud, slang-heavy, smug, poetic, or self-important.

The narrator is invisible. Do not address the audience, announce that a moment is satisfying or shocking, predict future developments, lecture about the theme, or explain why the writing works.

Do not write like a literary audiobook, trailer voice, screenplay, motivational essay, system manual, or settlement chronicle. A strong paragraph tells the viewer what changed, why it matters, and what pressure follows.

Most sentences should contain ten to twenty words, with an average near twelve to sixteen. Aim for roughly fifteen to thirty-two percent of sentences under nine words, not half the script.

Do not write more than three consecutive sentences under nine words unless a genuinely rapid action sequence requires it. Do not default to one sentence per paragraph. Most paragraphs should carry two to four closely related sentences, while one-sentence paragraphs are reserved for real reversals or impact.

Connect action, motive, response, and result in the same sentence when clarity improves. Use causal language naturally so the story feels like one event forcing the next.

Make causal relationships explicit whenever a viewer might otherwise wonder why an action worked, why a character changed plans, or why one result produced the next problem. This is a logic requirement, not a quota for connector words.

Create a meaningful turn approximately every two to four sentences. A turn is a decision, discovery, reversal, complication, consequence, reveal, or changed relationship.

Within the first five minutes, silently verify at least eighteen meaningful turns and no long block that only describes, explains, or repeats one unchanged situation.

Compress routine travel, repeated attribution, setup already understood, and low-value reaction. Slow down only for the betrayal, first proof, important mechanism, difficult choice, relationship boundary, climax, and payoff.

Avoid fragment chains, audiobook atmosphere, decorative scenery, repeated facial reactions, repetitive system windows, repeated shock crowds, summary followed by full replay, faux-profound narration, and repeated constructions such as for the first time or not this but that.

No paragraph should exist only to restate the moral, praise Joey, or explain a metaphor after the event already made the point.
