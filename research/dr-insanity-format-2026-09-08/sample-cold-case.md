## Visual and narration language

**Independently checked:** The 0:21 and 25:45 frames use large blue dialogue captions on solid black backing; sensitive terminology is visibly altered in the opening caption. At 23:32, the source image is an awkward close vehicle-interview view, with a hand in the foreground and the speaker partly cropped at the right edge: the edit retains imperfect footage while the account matters. The 38:16 frame adds a document/evidence visual rather than remaining on interview footage through financial explanation. The 46:18 frame geographically connects the closing account to the property. These are sampled treatments, not proof of a channel-wide style guide.

**Provider full-video observations, not independently confirmed throughout:** Desktop/media-player window motifs; satellite establishing shots; evidence-photo zooms and redactions; a low musical bed with source room tone; final outcome text and victim portraits. Audio descriptions are provider-only. Its description of the narrator as objective is too broad: the captions repeatedly assert what suspects believe or feel and interpret eye contact or demeanor. That dramatic technique should not become factual narration in Halderson.

The reusable pattern is **source encounter → short orientation → sustained answer → explanation of a contradiction → next source encounter**. The suspense comes from showing the account being tested. Narration frequently previews an impending reveal, sometimes for many minutes; use that sparingly so Halderson's evidence does not feel artificially withheld.

## Specific transfer to the Halderson paper edit

1. Give the cabin claim a source-audio moment, then date the July 7 report. Keep the audience clear about the claimed July 2 departure.
2. Use the cabin inspection as a concrete location change, supported by the separately checked source footage. A map explains distance; it does not prove a journey occurred.
3. Build the interview around complete exchanges. Insert evidence only when it answers or challenges what was just said, and clearly label evidence acquired after July 8.
4. Use simple document and map graphics grounded in our sources. Preserve resolution, original context and speaker identification rather than reproducing another channel's exact visual design.
5. End with the court-supported outcome and present appeal status. Do not infer guilt from demeanor, counsel requests or an investigator's confidence, and do not reproduce the sample's claims about an untried defendant as our own reporting.

This single sample supports a format hypothesis; it does not establish that the same proportions, graphics or opening length cause performance.
